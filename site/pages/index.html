THE LIVE DEMO

This is the real display, running on invented data and stepping through its scenes. On your wall, press and hold the screen for two seconds to open the settings.

What it shows

It's made to be glanced at from across the room, not read like a dashboard. It is not a monitoring tool: no alerts and no history. Keep your monitoring; this is the part you glance at. A problem takes over the scene, with a headline saying why. Small events play out while all is quiet. The colour of the words is the status: green when quiet, amber for a warning, red when critical, blue while something is at work.

  • An advert is blocked

    AdGuard Home, Pi-hole or Technitium

    A fly buzzes in and the knight swats it. Indoors, the cat catches it.

  • fail2ban bans someone

    fail2ban

    A goblin climbs over the castle wall, gets bonked on its saucepan and vanishes in a puff of smoke.

  • A download is imported

    Sonarr, Radarr or Lidarr

    A crate floats down on a parachute, with the title in the caption.

  • The Raspberry Pi goes quiet

    A second machine

    The raspberry pie is gone, down to its last slice, and the knight stares at the empty dish.

  • The CPU is pinned

    This machine

    The knight trains flat out in the yard, red in the face and sweating.

  • A container is unhealthy

    Docker

    The knight takes to bed with a thermometer, and the headline names the container.

  • A disk is filling up

    This machine

    Down in the cellar, the knight struggles with a heavy crate.

  • Someone opens an SSH session

    This machine

    The knight is called to court to talk with the monarch.

  • Updates are running

    A job you name, such as nightly maintenance

    The knight hammers at the anvil in the smithy.

  • A device goes down

    A NAS, a camera, a VM, anything with a port

    Each device stands in the scene as a medieval thing: an owl, a crystal ball, a potion. When one goes down it breaks, and a question mark blinks over it.

  • Something is streaming

    Plex, Jellyfin, Tautulli or Tracearr

    A television appears in whatever scene is showing, and the knight stops by now and then to watch it. The line under the headline says who is watching what.

  • The internet drops

    An address you choose

    The bell rope on the battlement is cut, and the knight is puzzled.

There's more: an attack, a backup that's late or has just finished, memory running low, and night, when the knight goes to bed and the screen dims. With nothing to report, the knight patrols the battlement, the camp, the house, the training yard and the smithy in turn.

The knight and the world

The look is chosen on the display itself. Press and hold the screen for two seconds and the settings open.

  • Three builds: Classic, Broad and Slim
  • Five weapons, from a greatsword to a crossbow that fires
  • Six skin tones, six hair colours and five hair styles
  • Six tabards and six plumes
  • Shields, helmets and armour, and a cloak or a cape
  • Three monarchs: a king, a queen and a monarch with no title
  • A calico cat or a ginger tabby

You also choose which quiet scenes take turns, and where some problems play out: running hot by the camp fire instead of in the yard, say.

A world sets the scenes, the cast, the words and their colours in one go. The medieval world is the one included. The pack format is documented for the curious; a normal page loads only packs the project has signed.

What it reads

Built-in providers for the services most homelabs run, and a way to read anything else that answers with JSON. A small collector does the reading and writes one file; the display only reads that file. Your keys stay in a .env file on your machine, and each goes only to its own service. The collector only reads, but several services offer no read-only key, so .env may hold a login that could change them. Treat it as you treat those services' own configuration.

  • Streams playing

    • Tautulli
    • Plex Media Server
    • Jellyfin
    • Tracearr

  • Adverts blocked

    • AdGuard Home
    • Pi-hole 6
    • Technitium DNS Server

  • Downloads imported

    • Sonarr
    • Radarr
    • Lidarr
    • more than one of each

  • Bans

    • fail2ban, from its own database

  • This machine

    • CPU
    • Memory
    • Disks
    • Backup log
    • Updates running
    • SSH sessions

  • The network

    • Internet
    • DNS
    • Up to 32 devices
    • Docker containers, through a read-only Docker API proxy that you run (the compose file has none, and the collector gets no Docker socket)

  • Anything else

    A generic check reads one value from any address that answers with JSON, tests it, and shows your own words when the test holds. For a whole service, write a provider file: it's data, read by the same rules as the built-in ones, and never runs as code.

Quick start

knightwatcher-io/knightwatcher

Three steps, one of them a permissions step, then you add the services you run.

What you need, all of it tested:

  • Linux with Docker and its compose plugin, and the acl package. Tested on x86-64. ARM and the Raspberry Pi are untested.
  • Two containers from one image. They use about 35 MB of memory between them and almost no CPU while idle.
  • A modern browser on the display, a tablet's for example.

  1. Get the files and make the folders

    git clone https://github.com/knightwatcher-io/knightwatcher
cd knightwatcher
cp config.example.toml config.toml
cp .env.example .env && chmod 600 .env
mkdir -p var/out var/server
setfacl -m u:10001:rwx var var/out var/server
setfacl -d -m u:10001:rwx,u:$(id -u):rwx var var/out var/server

    The containers run as uid 10001; the last two lines let them write to those folders.

  2. Point it at two things

    In .env, set KW_BACKUP_LOG to your backup log and KW_DATA_DISK to any folder on the disk to watch. Both must exist. No backup log yet? Run touch var/backup.log and use ./var/backup.log.

  3. Start it

    docker compose up -d

    Compose pulls ghcr.io/knightwatcher-io/knightwatcher and runs the collector and the display's server from it. Open http://<this machine>:8080/: the knight appears at once, and the first readings within a minute.

The example config turns on the sources that read this machine, plus an internet check and a DNS check. Each service is written out in config.toml but commented: remove the #, add your address, and put its key in .env.

The display has no login, and a streams service shows who is watching what (show = "count" hides the names). Docker publishes it on every address of the host. Set KW_SERVER_ADDRESS=127.0.0.1 to keep it to the machine, behind a reverse proxy with a login. The admin password covers the admin page only. Never put the display on the internet without a login.

Usage counts are off unless you turn them on. If you are happy to help, set KW_ANALYTICS=on in .env: what leaves your network.

What leaves your network

Two things at most, and nothing else.

The usage counts

If you turn them on, the collector sends one small report at start and every six hours: a random install id, the version, the install's age and uptime, the world and look you chose, which built-in sources are on, how many imports and checks you have, and how many displays opened the page in the last six hours. The receiver adds the day and the country Cloudflare sees. No name, address, title or credential is in it. The receiver runs on Cloudflare, keeps no IP address, and its code is in the repository's telemetry/ folder. They stay off unless .env says KW_ANALYTICS=on; with off, or the line left out, nothing is sent.

Two checks

The example config asks https://1.1.1.1/cdn-cgi/trace (Cloudflare's) whether the internet is up, and asks your DNS server (the example uses the host's own resolver) to look up example.com. Both addresses are in config.toml, and either check can be removed there.

Past that, the collector contacts only the addresses you give it, and the display and the server contact nothing outside your network.
